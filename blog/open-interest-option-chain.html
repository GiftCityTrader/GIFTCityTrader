Open Interest and the Option Chain: What the Data Really Shows

What open interest measures, how to combine OI change with price change, how to read an option chain honestly, and why max pain and PCR explain less than most people claim.

By the Gift City Trader team Updated 16 April 2026 12 min read

What's inside

  1. What open interest actually counts
  2. Combining OI change with price change
  3. Reading an option chain properly
  4. Put-Call Ratio and how it misleads

Open interest is the most confidently misused number in Indian retail trading. Every afternoon, somewhere, a chart appears showing "highest OI at 24,000 CE — strong resistance", followed by a confident directional call. Sometimes it works. Often it doesn't. And the people posting it usually cannot explain what would have to be true for the interpretation to hold.

Open interest is genuinely useful data. It is also ambiguous by construction — the same number is produced by opposite intentions. This guide covers what OI measures, what it cannot tell you, and how to read an option chain without inventing a story.

What open interest actually counts

Open interest is the number of contracts currently outstanding — positions that have been opened and not yet closed. It is not volume, and confusing the two is the first error.

VolumeOpen interest
MeasuresContracts traded during the periodContracts still open right now
ResetsEvery dayCarries forward until positions close
Tells youActivityCommitment

The mechanics, which make the ambiguity obvious:

  • A new buyer meets a new seller → OI increases by one.
  • An existing holder sells to a new buyer → OI unchanged (the position moved hands).
  • An existing buyer closes against an existing seller → OI decreases.

Every contract has a buyer and a seller. So when OI rises by 10,000 at a strike, exactly 10,000 new long contracts and 10,000 new short contracts were created. OI never tells you which side was the aggressor. Any claim it does is an assumption dressed as data.

Combining OI change with price change

OI on its own is nearly meaningless. Paired with the price move over the same period, it becomes a reasonable inference about what kind of activity is occurring. This four-way table is the standard framework, applied here to futures:

PriceOpen interestReasonable interpretation
RisingRisingLong build-up — new money entering on the long side. The move has fresh participation behind it.
FallingRisingShort build-up — new positions being created on the short side.
RisingFallingShort covering — shorts closing out. The rise is driven by exits, not conviction, and often fades.
FallingFallingLong unwinding — longs giving up. Selling pressure from exits rather than new bears.

The practically valuable distinction is between the two rising-price rows. A rally on rising OI has new buyers committing capital. A rally on falling OI is people who were short buying back to escape — and once they have all escaped, the buying stops. Short-covering rallies are fast, look impressive and frequently give everything back.

Use it as context, not as a signal These readings describe what has already happened. They tell you the character of a move — whether it has fuel — not what happens next. Treat OI as a quality check on a setup you found on the chart, never as the setup itself.

Reading an option chain properly

An option chain lists every available strike with its calls on one side and puts on the other. For each you typically get last traded price, volume, open interest, change in OI, and implied volatility. Read it in this order.

1. Change in OI, not total OI

Total OI is largely history — it accumulated over weeks and includes hedges, arbitrage and institutional structures that have nothing to do with today's direction. Change in OI since the previous close is where today's information is. A strike with huge total OI but no change today is telling you about last week.

2. Where is the change concentrated?

Look for the two or three strikes with unusually large OI additions. Those are the prices where participants are actively taking new positions today.

3. Now the honest caveat

The standard retail interpretation is: heavy call OI above spot = resistance (because option sellers there expect price to stay below), heavy put OI below spot = support. This holds often enough to be useful, for a real reason: option sellers at those strikes hedge their exposure, and that hedging creates genuine order flow around those levels.

But it fails in identifiable situations, and knowing them is what separates useful analysis from chart-astrology:

  • Buyers, not sellers, may be building. Heavy call OI can equally mean aggressive call buying — a bullish signal read backwards as resistance.
  • Much of it is hedging. An institution with a large equity portfolio buying index puts adds enormous put OI and has no directional view on the index at all.
  • Levels move. On a trending day, positions at the "resistance" strike are unwound and rebuilt higher within hours. Yesterday's max-OI strike can be irrelevant by lunch.
  • It says nothing about timing. Even when the level holds, it might hold after a 200-point excursion through it.

Put-Call Ratio: what it is and how it misleads

PCR is put OI divided by call OI. High PCR is conventionally called bullish, low PCR bearish, on a contrarian basis — the logic being that when everyone has bought puts, there is nobody left to sell.

Two problems with using it casually. First, the extremes are what matter, not the middle; a PCR of 0.95 versus 1.05 is noise, and reading direction into that is meaningless. Second, the "normal" range differs by instrument and by market regime, so a single fixed threshold cannot work across instruments.

Used properly it is a sentiment gauge at extremes and nothing more: when PCR reaches a level rarely seen in the past year, positioning is crowded, and crowded positioning is fragile. That is the whole claim. Anything more precise is over-reading.

Max pain, and why it explains less than it seems

Max pain is the strike at which the total value of outstanding options that expire worthless is maximised — loosely, the price causing the greatest aggregate loss to option buyers. The folklore is that price gravitates toward it on expiry day.

There is a partial mechanical basis: as expiry nears, option sellers hedging their positions do buy and sell the underlying in ways that can dampen movement — a real effect known as gamma pinning. But max pain is a single number computed from a distribution that changes continuously, it ignores who is hedged and who is not, and in a genuinely trending or news-driven session it has no predictive value whatsoever. It is an interesting statistic, not a trading plan.

A practical way to use this data

A workflow that respects what OI can and cannot tell you:

  1. Find your levels on the chart first. Support and resistance from price structure, drawn before you open the option chain.
  2. Open the chain and look at change in OI. Do the strikes with today's largest OI additions sit near your levels? Agreement between independent methods is meaningful.
  3. Check the futures OI-and-price table. Is the current move a fresh build-up or an unwind? A rally on short covering deserves a smaller position and a tighter target.
  4. Check IV. If you are buying options, elevated IV means you need a bigger move to profit.
  5. Decide from price. Size from your stop. The chain informs the decision; it never makes it.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Treating max-OI strikes as walls. They are areas of interest. Price passes through them regularly.
  • Reading total OI instead of change. Old positioning masquerading as today's signal.
  • Assuming the seller's view. Every contract has both sides. You are guessing which one was aggressive.
  • Using OI on illiquid stock options. Outside a handful of large caps and the indices, Indian stock option chains are too thin for the data to mean much.
  • Building an entire strategy on it. OI is a supporting instrument. It has never been the melody.

Used with that discipline, the option chain adds genuine information about where participation is concentrated. Used as a crystal ball, it produces very confident, very wrong forecasts — which is exactly what you see on social media every afternoon.
