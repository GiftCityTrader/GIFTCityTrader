Risk Management and Position Sizing for Indian Traders

The arithmetic of drawdown, the 1% rule, the position sizing formula, where stop-losses belong, portfolio-level limits and the journal that makes it all verifiable.

Ask a room of new traders what they want to learn and almost all of them will say entries. Ask traders who have survived five years and they will talk about size. That gap is the whole subject of this article.

Here is the uncomfortable arithmetic that makes it unavoidable: a 50% loss requires a 100% gain to recover. A 75% loss requires 300%. The damage curve is not symmetric, and it is why capital preservation is not a conservative preference — it is a mathematical requirement for staying in the game long enough for any edge to show up.

The arithmetic of drawdown

Capital lostGain needed to get back to breakeven
10%11.1%
25%33.3%
50%100%
75%300%
90%900%

Notice where the curve turns vicious. Up to about 20%, recovery is realistic. Past 50%, you need a performance you have almost certainly never produced. Most blown accounts are not the result of one catastrophic trade — they are the result of a series of ordinary losses taken at a size that never allowed recovery.

Everything below exists to keep you on the flat part of that curve.

The 1% rule, and what it actually means

The standard professional guideline: risk no more than 1% of trading capital on a single trade. Newer traders often use 0.5%; nobody serious uses 5%.

The critical distinction — and the one beginners consistently miss — is that this is 1% of capital at risk, not 1% of capital deployed.

Deployed versus at risk With ₹5,00,000 capital, buying ₹1,00,000 of a stock deploys 20% of capital. But if your stop-loss is 5% below entry, the amount at risk is ₹5,000 — which is 1% of capital. The position size is large; the risk is controlled. Confusing these two numbers is why people either over-trade or refuse to take reasonable positions.

Why 1%? Because it makes a losing streak survivable. Ten consecutive losses at 1% costs you roughly 10% of capital — unpleasant, entirely recoverable, and psychologically bearable. Ten consecutive losses at 5% costs about 40%, which needs a 67% gain to repair and will almost certainly break your discipline before it breaks your account.

And ten consecutive losses are not unusual. Any strategy with a 50% win rate will produce a run of ten losers reasonably often over a few hundred trades. Plan for the streak, because it is coming.

Position sizing: the formula

Once the stop-loss and the risk limit are fixed, position size is not a judgement call. It is arithmetic:

Position size = (Capital × Risk %) ÷ (Entry price − Stop-loss price)

Worked example — equity

  • Capital: ₹5,00,000
  • Risk per trade: 1% = ₹5,000
  • Entry: ₹850, stop-loss: ₹820 → risk per share = ₹30
  • Position size = 5,000 ÷ 30 = 166 shares (about ₹1,41,100 deployed)

Now change one thing. Same trade, but the sensible stop is at ₹800 instead — risk per share becomes ₹50, and position size drops to 100 shares. A wider stop means a smaller position, not more risk. This is the mechanism that lets you use a stop where it logically belongs rather than where it happens to be convenient.

Worked example — futures and options

Lot sizes remove your freedom to fine-tune, which makes the calculation more important, not less. If one lot of a Nifty future implies ₹12,000 of risk to your stop and your 1% limit is ₹5,000, the honest answer is that this trade is not available to you. Not "take it anyway and use a tighter stop that does not match the chart" — skip it, or trade a smaller instrument.

Being unable to take a setup because the size does not fit is normal and correct. Forcing it is how accounts end.

Where a stop-loss actually belongs

A stop is not a monetary preference. It is the price at which your reason for the trade stops being true.

  • Structure-based. Below the swing low that defines your setup, or beyond the support zone. If price goes there, the pattern has failed — no interpretation needed.
  • Volatility-based. A multiple of ATR (Average True Range) below entry. This adapts automatically: wider in volatile conditions, tighter in calm ones, so you are not stopped out by ordinary noise.
  • Time-based. "If this has not worked within three sessions, I am out." Capital tied up in a position going nowhere has an opportunity cost, and for option buyers it also has a theta cost.

What a stop should never be: a round number chosen because it feels safe, or a level moved further away as price approaches it. Moving a stop against yourself is the mechanical step by which a planned 1% loss becomes an unplanned 8% one.

Risk-reward, and the win rate it demands

Risk-reward is the ratio of what you stand to gain to what you stand to lose. Its real function is to tell you what win rate you need to survive:

Risk : RewardBreakeven win rateComfortable win rate
1 : 150%60%+
1 : 233%40%+
1 : 325%33%+
2 : 167%75%+

At 1:3 you can be wrong two times out of three and still make money. That is why the target should be set by what the chart plausibly offers — the next resistance zone, the measured move of the pattern — rather than by a number you would like to earn. If the realistic target sits closer than your stop, the trade is not worth taking regardless of how confident you feel.

Note also that the breakeven column ignores costs. Add brokerage, STT, GST and slippage, and the required win rate rises — significantly so for high-frequency intraday strategies.

Portfolio-level risk

Per-trade risk is necessary but not sufficient. Five simultaneous positions at 1% each is not 1% risk — and if all five are banking stocks, it is effectively a single 5% bet on one sector.

Three limits worth writing down:

  1. Total open risk cap. The sum of all live stop distances should not exceed roughly 3–5% of capital. When you are at the cap, you take no new trades until something closes.
  2. Correlation cap. Positions in the same sector, or long index futures plus long three index-heavy stocks, are one position wearing several names. Count them as one.
  3. Daily loss limit. A fixed number — commonly 2–3% of capital — at which you stop trading for the day. Not "after one more attempt to get it back". The single worst hour of most traders' months is the hour after they hit their limit and kept going.

Managing a drawdown

Every strategy has losing periods. What separates traders who continue from traders who quit is having decided the response in advance, while calm:

  • At 5% down from your peak: nothing. This is normal variance. Do not change anything — most strategy-hopping starts here and is the real cause of the next drawdown.
  • At 10% down: halve your position size and review your journal. Are you taking your defined setups, or improvising? Usually it is the latter.
  • At 15–20% down: stop. Go to paper trading or minimum size until you produce a run of trades that follow your own rules. The market will still be here.

Reducing size during a drawdown is not defeatism. It is what allows the recovery to happen at all, because smaller losses need smaller gains to repair.

The journal that makes this real

None of the above is verifiable without a record. A journal that works needs, for every trade: date and instrument, entry, stop, target, position size, risk in rupees, the reason for entry written before the trade, the outcome, and — the most valuable column — whether you followed your plan, scored yes or no independently of profit.

That last column is the one that produces improvement, because it separates two things that feel identical in the moment: a good decision with a bad outcome, and a bad decision that got lucky. Over a hundred trades, traders discover something consistent and uncomfortable — most of their losses come from a small number of "no" trades, taken outside their own rules, usually after a loss or after a big win.

You cannot control whether a trade wins. You can control how much it costs when it loses. Only one of those is where an edge comes from.

A pre-trade checklist

  1. What is my entry, and why — in one sentence?
  2. Where does this idea become wrong? That is my stop.
  3. How many shares or lots does that stop allow at 1% risk?
  4. What is the realistic target, and is the ratio at least 1:2?
  5. How much total risk is already open across my positions?
  6. Have I hit my daily loss limit?
  7. Am I taking this because it meets my rules, or because I want to be doing something?

Seven questions, under a minute. It is the cheapest risk management available, and it is the part almost nobody does.
