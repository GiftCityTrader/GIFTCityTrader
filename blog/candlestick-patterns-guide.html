Candlestick Patterns: What Each Candle Is Actually Telling You

A practical guide to candlestick patterns for Indian traders — what a candle encodes, the dozen patterns worth knowing, and the four context filters that decide whether a pattern works.

By the Gift City Trader team Updated 3 February 2026 13 min read

Candlestick charts are the first thing every new trader learns and the last thing most of them learn properly. The usual route is a downloaded PDF with sixty pattern names, memorised over a weekend, applied on Monday, abandoned by Friday. The patterns did not fail. The missing piece was always the same: a candle is a record of a fight, and the pattern only matters if you know where on the chart the fight happened.

This guide covers what a candle encodes, the dozen or so patterns worth knowing, and — more importantly — the filters that separate a pattern that works from an identical-looking one that doesn't.

What one candle actually encodes

A single candle compresses four numbers from a fixed slice of time: the open, the high, the low and the close.

  • The thick part is the body — the distance between open and close. It is coloured one way if the close was above the open (buyers won the session) and the other way if below (sellers won).
  • The thin lines above and below are the wicks or shadows — the extremes that were reached and then rejected.

The interpretation follows directly from that, and you can derive every pattern from these three sentences rather than memorising them:

  1. A long body means one side dominated the whole session. Conviction.
  2. A long wick means price went there and got pushed back. Rejection.
  3. A small body with long wicks on both sides means both sides tried and neither won. Indecision.

That is the entire vocabulary. A "hammer" is not a magic shape; it is a session where price fell hard, buyers absorbed all of it, and the close came back near the top. The name is just shorthand.

Timeframe changes the meaning

A hammer on a 5-minute chart describes twenty minutes of noise. A hammer on a weekly chart describes five days during which institutions absorbed everything sellers could throw at a level. They look identical and mean completely different things. As a rule: the higher the timeframe, the more capital and more decisions are compressed into the candle, and the more weight it deserves.

Single-candle patterns worth knowing

Doji

Open and close are almost identical, producing a body that is a thin line. Neither side achieved anything. A doji in the middle of a range is meaningless — indecision inside indecision. A doji after an extended one-way run is a genuine signal: the trend just stopped making progress for the first time.

Hammer and hanging man

Small body near the top of the range, long lower wick at least twice the body, little or no upper wick. Price was rejected downward.

The same shape has two names depending on context. After a downtrend, at a level buyers care about, it is a hammer — a potential bottom. After an uptrend, it is a hanging man — a warning that selling has appeared where there was none. Identical candle, opposite implication. This is the clearest possible proof that location beats shape.

Shooting star and inverted hammer

The mirror image: small body near the bottom, long upper wick. Price rallied and was rejected. At the top of a move it is a shooting star (bearish); at the bottom of a move it is an inverted hammer (possible bullish exhaustion, but weaker and usually needs the next candle to confirm).

Marubozu

A full body with essentially no wicks. Price opened at one extreme and closed at the other, never looking back. This is the strongest single-candle statement of conviction there is. A bullish marubozu breaking out of a range is worth far more attention than most named reversal patterns.

Two-candle patterns

Bullish and bearish engulfing

The second candle's body completely covers the first candle's body, in the opposite direction. A bullish engulfing after a decline says that in one session buyers erased everything the previous session's sellers achieved.

Quality checks that most people skip: the second candle's body should be meaningfully larger, not larger by two paise; volume on the engulfing candle should exceed the recent average; and it should appear at a level, not in mid-air. An engulfing candle in the middle of a range is noise.

Piercing line and dark cloud cover

Weaker cousins of engulfing. Price gaps against the prior candle, then reverses to close beyond the midpoint of that candle's body — but not past its full range. Piercing line is the bullish version, dark cloud cover the bearish. Treat them as "engulfing, but only 60% convinced".

Harami

The opposite geometry: a large candle followed by a small one entirely inside its range. The name is Japanese for "pregnant". It signals not reversal but deceleration — the trend has stopped expanding. Useful as a heads-up to tighten a stop, poor as a standalone entry.

Tweezer top and bottom

Two or more candles rejecting from almost exactly the same price. What makes this work has nothing to do with the candles and everything to do with the repetition: the market tested one price twice and failed twice. That is a level.

Three-candle patterns

Morning star and evening star

The most reliable of the classical reversals, because the structure encodes an entire narrative in three sessions:

  1. A large candle in the direction of the existing trend — the trend still looks healthy.
  2. A small-bodied candle, often gapping away — momentum stalls; nobody is in control.
  3. A large candle in the opposite direction, closing well into the first candle's body — the other side takes over decisively.

The third candle is the one that matters. If it closes past the midpoint of candle one, the pattern has teeth. If it closes barely into it, you have a shape, not a signal.

Three white soldiers / three black crows

Three consecutive strong candles in the same direction, each opening within the previous body and closing near its high (or low). It confirms a trend rather than reversing one. The practical caution: by the time three full candles have printed, you are often late. Better used as evidence that a breakout is real than as an entry trigger itself.

The four filters that make patterns work

Here is the honest version of candlestick trading. Any pattern in isolation is close to a coin flip. What creates an edge is the filter stack applied on top. Run every candidate through these four questions.

1. Is it at a level?

A reversal pattern is only meaningful where a reversal is plausible — a prior swing high or low, a well-tested support, a moving average that price has respected, the edge of a range, a round number that the market clearly watches. The same hammer is a trade at a level and a lottery ticket in the middle of nowhere.

2. What did volume do?

A reversal candle should carry above-average volume. That is the footprint of size changing hands. A perfect-looking engulfing candle on the lowest volume of the week usually means very little happened — a few retail orders shifted a thin book.

3. Which way is the higher timeframe pointing?

A bullish pattern on the 15-minute chart inside a clean daily downtrend is fighting the current. It can work; it works less often and pays less when it does. Check the timeframe one or two steps above the one you trade before you take any counter-trend signal.

4. Where is your invalidation?

Every candlestick pattern comes with a free stop-loss built in: the extreme of the pattern. Below the hammer's low. Above the shooting star's high. If price goes there, the story the candle told was wrong — no interpretation required. If that stop is so far away that a sensible position size becomes absurdly small, the trade is not available to you. Skip it.

A useful discipline Before entering on any pattern, write one sentence: "I am buying because sellers were rejected at <level> on above-average volume, and I am wrong below <price>." If you cannot complete that sentence, the setup is not clear enough to trade.

Common mistakes with candlesticks

  • Trading the pattern before the candle closes. A beautiful hammer at 2:40 p.m. can be an ugly bearish candle at 3:30. Until the period closes, the pattern does not exist.
  • Pattern hunting. If you scan hard enough you will find a pattern on every chart in every session. Decide your level first, then look at the candles that print there — not the reverse.
  • Ignoring the gap. On Indian equities, overnight gaps distort daily patterns. Many textbook formations from Japanese rice markets assume continuous trading.
  • Using them alone on illiquid stocks. In a thin small-cap, a single large order creates a textbook candle that reflects one participant, not market sentiment.
  • Memorising sixty patterns. Professionals use maybe eight. Depth beats breadth.

How to actually learn this

Reading about candles produces recognition, not skill. The bridge is repetition with feedback:

  1. Pick one liquid instrument — Nifty, Bank Nifty, or a large-cap you follow.
  2. Open a daily chart and mark the obvious horizontal levels first, with no candles in mind.
  3. Scroll back one year. At each level, note which candles printed and what happened over the next five sessions. Record it in a sheet: pattern, level quality, volume, outcome.
  4. After a hundred observations you will have your own statistics — and they will be more useful than any published win rate, because they are drawn from the instrument you actually trade.

That exercise takes a few evenings. It is the difference between knowing the names and reading the chart.
