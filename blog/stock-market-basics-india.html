Stock Market Basics in India: The Guide You Should Read Before Your First Trade

How the Indian stock market actually works — NSE and BSE, SEBI, demat accounts, order types, real trading costs, and a sane 90-day plan for a complete beginner.

Almost every new trader in India starts at the same place: someone in the family, or a reel on the phone, mentions that the market is running well. An app gets downloaded, a KYC gets completed, and within a week money is riding on a stock whose business nobody in the room could describe. That sequence is exactly backwards, and it is the single most reliable way to lose your first ₹50,000.

This guide fixes the order. It walks through what the Indian stock market actually is, who the players are, what happens between the moment you tap Buy and the moment shares land in your account, and the handful of costs and rules that quietly decide whether you finish the year up or down. No jargon is used without being explained first.

What a stock market really is

A company that wants to grow needs money. It has two honest options: borrow it (debt), or sell a slice of the ownership (equity). When it sells ownership, it splits itself into a large number of identical units called shares. Buy one share and you own that fraction of the business — its factories, its brand, its future profits and its future problems.

The stock market is simply the organised place where those ownership slices change hands. It has two distinct halves, and confusing them causes a lot of beginner mistakes:

  • Primary market. The company itself sells new shares to the public for the first time — an IPO (Initial Public Offering). The money you pay goes to the company.
  • Secondary market. Everything after that. You buy from another investor who wants out. The company receives nothing; it is a transfer between two shareholders. Well over 99% of daily activity is secondary market trading.

So when you buy Reliance shares on a Tuesday afternoon, Reliance does not get your money. Some other person or fund who decided to sell does. That is worth internalising, because it reframes what a price actually is: not an official valuation, but the last number two strangers agreed on.

Where a share price comes from

There is no committee setting prices. The exchange runs a continuous order book: every buy order sits on one side with the price the buyer is willing to pay (the bid), every sell order sits on the other with the price the seller wants (the ask). When the highest bid meets the lowest ask, a trade happens, and that trade price becomes "the price".

This is why prices move on news, on rumours, and sometimes on nothing at all. Price is a live vote on what a business is worth, taken every microsecond by people with wildly different information, time horizons and emotional states.

Who runs the show: exchanges, SEBI, and the depositories

NSE and BSE

India has two main exchanges. The BSE (Bombay Stock Exchange, founded 1875) is Asia's oldest and lists the largest number of companies; its headline index is the Sensex, made of 30 large companies. The NSE (National Stock Exchange, 1992) introduced fully electronic trading to India and today carries the overwhelming majority of volume, especially in derivatives; its headline index is the Nifty 50.

For a retail trader the practical differences are small — most large stocks are listed on both, and your broker routes to whichever offers a better price. Where it matters is derivatives: index options on Nifty and Bank Nifty are an NSE product, and that is where most of India's F&O activity lives.

SEBI

The Securities and Exchange Board of India is the regulator. It licenses brokers, sets margin rules, polices insider trading and market manipulation, and decides what a company must disclose to the public. When you hear that lot sizes changed, or that intraday leverage was reduced, or that weekly expiries were trimmed — that is SEBI.

One SEBI rule matters personally to you: anyone who gives individual, paid buy/sell advice must be a registered Investment Adviser or Research Analyst. Education about how markets work does not require registration; telling you to buy a specific stock at a specific price does. If somebody is charging for calls without a registration number, that is a legal problem for them and a risk for you.

NSDL, CDSL and your broker

Shares have not existed on paper since the late 1990s. They sit as electronic entries in a depository — India has two, NSDL and CDSL. Your demat account is your folder inside one of them.

Your broker is the intermediary that lets you place orders on the exchange and maintains that demat account on your behalf. This distinction matters more than people realise: your shares are held at the depository under your own unique ID, not inside the broker. If your broker shuts down tomorrow, your shares still exist. You can verify this yourself — CDSL and NSDL email you a monthly holding statement directly, independent of your broker's app. Read it.

The three accounts you need

AccountWhat it holdsWho provides it
Bank accountYour moneyYour bank
Trading accountNothing — it is the channel that carries orders to the exchangeYour broker

Opening all three is now a same-day online process: PAN, Aadhaar-linked mobile, a bank proof, a signature image and a short video verification. Nobody should be charging you a large sum to "open an account for you".

Check before you sign Compare the annual maintenance charge (AMC) on the demat account, the brokerage per order, and the DP charges — a flat fee, typically around ₹13–25, deducted every time you sell from your demat. Frequent small sells make DP charges surprisingly expensive.

Order types, and the one that will save you

Your broker's app offers several order types. Four cover almost everything:

Market order

"Buy me 50 shares, at whatever the market is right now." It fills immediately. The risk is slippage: in an illiquid stock or a fast-moving minute, the price you get can be meaningfully worse than the one on screen. Reasonable in Nifty 50 stocks during normal hours; dangerous in a small-cap at 9:15 a.m.

Limit order

"Buy me 50 shares, but not above ₹242.50." It only fills at your price or better. It might not fill at all. For anything outside the most liquid names, this should be your default.

Stop-loss (SL) and stop-loss market (SL-M)

This is the order that decides whether you survive your first bad year. A stop-loss sits dormant until price hits a trigger, at which point it becomes a live order to exit.

Say you buy at ₹500 and decide you are wrong below ₹485. You place an SL with trigger ₹486 and limit price ₹484. If price falls to ₹486, a sell order at ₹484 goes into the book. SL-M does the same but sends a market order instead — it guarantees exit but not price.

The gap risk nobody mentions A stop-loss protects you during trading hours. It cannot protect you against an overnight gap. If a stock closes at ₹500 and opens at ₹430 after bad news, your ₹486 stop triggers at ₹430, not ₹486. This is precisely why position size, not the stop alone, is your real risk control.

Equity, futures and options — three very different games

New traders often move from delivery equity into F&O within weeks, attracted by leverage, without registering that they have changed sport entirely.

Can you lose more than you put in?NoPossible in a gapYes, on short options and futures

SEBI's own studies of individual traders in the equity derivatives segment have repeatedly found that a large majority — consistently in the region of nine out of ten — lose money over a financial year, and that the average loss is not small. That is not a reason to never trade derivatives. It is a reason to treat the transition as a deliberate, prepared step rather than a Tuesday impulse.

The costs that quietly eat your returns

Beginners compare brokerage and stop there. Brokerage is often the smallest line. A single equity intraday trade in India can attract:

  • Brokerage — flat per order at discount brokers, or a percentage at full-service ones.
  • STT (Securities Transaction Tax) — charged on the sell side for intraday, both sides for delivery.
  • Exchange transaction charges — a small percentage of turnover.
  • SEBI turnover fee and stamp duty.
  • GST at 18%, levied on brokerage plus transaction charges.
  • DP charges on every delivery sell.

Individually trivial. Together, for someone taking six or eight intraday trades a day, they can consume a very large share of gross profit. Before you decide a strategy "works", run it through your broker's actual contract note, not a spreadsheet of raw price moves. Many strategies that look profitable on paper are net negative after costs — and finding that out on paper is far cheaper than finding it out live.

Tax, in one paragraph

Broadly: equity held over a year is long-term capital gains, held under a year is short-term capital gains, and intraday plus F&O is treated as business income taxed at your slab rate. Business income also means you can set off expenses and carry forward losses, but it brings bookkeeping and possibly audit obligations. Rates and thresholds change with each Budget, so confirm current numbers with a chartered accountant rather than with a forum post.

A sane first ninety days

If you are starting now, this sequence has a far better survival rate than the usual one:

  1. Weeks 1–2 — read, don't trade. Learn to read a candlestick chart, understand what an index is, and follow five companies whose products you actually use.
  2. Weeks 3–4 — paper trade. Write down entry, stop-loss, target and reason for twenty hypothetical trades. Score them honestly. Most people discover their "obvious" setups are coin flips.
  3. Weeks 5–8 — trade tiny. Real money, but an amount whose total loss would annoy you rather than hurt you. The purpose is not profit; it is to feel what fear does to your decision-making.
  4. Weeks 9–12 — audit. Review the journal. Which setups made money? Which broke discipline? Only now consider increasing size — and only on the setups that earned it.

Nothing in that plan is exciting, which is exactly why it works. The market pays for patience and charges heavily for impatience.

Seven mistakes that cost beginners the most

  1. Averaging down without a plan. Adding to a losing position to "reduce average price" turns a small controlled loss into an uncontrolled one.
  2. Trading on tips. A tip has no stop-loss, no position size and no accountability. When it goes wrong, nobody picks up the phone.
  3. Confusing a fall with a discount. A stock down 60% can fall another 60%. Price alone tells you nothing about value.
  4. Using leverage before consistency. Leverage multiplies whatever edge you have. If your edge is negative, leverage just gets you there faster.
  5. No journal. Without a written record you will remember your wins and quietly forget your losses. Every trader does this.
  6. Revenge trading. The trade taken immediately after a painful loss is statistically the worst trade most people make all week.
  7. Risking money with a job to do. Rent, fees and emergency funds have no business in a trading account. Money you cannot afford to lose makes you trade badly, which then makes you lose it.

Where to go next

Once the plumbing above is clear, the next skill is reading price itself — what a candle is telling you and where a market is likely to pause. That is the natural second step, and it is the subject of the candlestick and support/resistance guides linked below.

Take it slowly. The market has been open for 150 years and it will still be there next month.
