Fundamental Analysis: Reading a Company's Numbers Without an Accounting Degree

The three financial statements, the six ratios that matter, why cash flow settles arguments, promoter pledging, and a practical screening routine for Indian listed companies.

Technical analysis tells you when the market is willing to pay more. Fundamental analysis tells you whether the thing being paid for is worth anything. Long-term investors need the second; traders who hold positions for weeks need at least enough of it to avoid being long a company whose accounts are falling apart.

You do not need to be a chartered accountant. You need to be able to open a company's annual report, find six or seven numbers, and know what a bad answer looks like. This guide is that shortlist.

The three statements, in three paragraphs

The profit and loss statement covers a period — a quarter or a year. Revenue at the top, costs subtracted step by step, profit at the bottom. It answers: did the business make money over this stretch?

The balance sheet is a snapshot on one date. Assets on one side, liabilities and equity on the other, always equal. It answers: what does the business own, what does it owe, and how much of it belongs to shareholders?

The cash flow statement tracks actual cash movement across three buckets — operating, investing and financing. It answers the question that matters most: did the profit reported on the P&L actually arrive as money?

Which one to read first Cash flow. Profit involves judgement about when to recognise revenue and how to spread costs. Cash is harder to dress up. A company reporting rising profits alongside consistently negative operating cash flow is the single most useful warning sign available to a non-specialist.

Reading the P&L without being misled

Work down the statement and ask one question at each level.

  • Revenue. Is it growing? More importantly, is growth coming from selling more, or from acquiring companies, or from a one-off? The management discussion section usually says.
  • Operating profit (EBITDA) margin. Operating profit divided by revenue. Compare it to the same company three years ago and to its competitors — not to companies in other industries, where the normal range is completely different.
  • Interest cost. Rising interest against flat operating profit means debt is consuming the business.
  • Net profit. Check whether it was boosted by "other income" — the sale of an asset, a tax writeback, a treasury gain. Those do not repeat, and a stock re-rated on non-repeating income tends to give it back.

The pattern to look for

Steady revenue growth with stable or improving margins is a business with pricing power. Revenue growth with shrinking margins usually means it is buying growth by cutting price — much less valuable, and often temporary.

The balance sheet: three checks

Debt to equity

Total borrowings divided by shareholders' equity. Below 0.5 is comfortable for most manufacturing and consumer businesses; above 1 needs a reason; above 2 means the business is running on lenders' money and small setbacks become existential.

The important caveat: banks and non-banking finance companies are supposed to be leveraged — borrowing and lending is their business model. Applying an industrial company's debt standard to a bank is a category error, and it is one of the most common mistakes beginners make with screeners.

Receivables and inventory

If revenue grows 15% but receivables grow 45%, the company is booking sales that customers have not paid for. If inventory grows much faster than sales, product is not moving. Both are early signals, and both are visible in the balance sheet long before they show up in profit.

Promoter pledging

Specific to Indian markets and worth checking every time. Promoters can pledge their own shares as collateral for personal loans. If the price falls, lenders sell those shares, which pushes the price down further. High pledging — anything above roughly 25% of promoter holding — has preceded a large share of India's worst retail investing outcomes. The shareholding pattern is filed quarterly with the exchanges and is free to view.

Six ratios worth knowing

RatioWhat it asksHow to read it
P/EPrice per rupee of earningsOnly meaningful against the same company's history and its direct peers. A high P/E is not "expensive" if growth justifies it.
P/BPrice per rupee of book valueUseful for banks and asset-heavy businesses; nearly meaningless for asset-light software or services firms.
ROEProfit generated per rupee of shareholder equityConsistently above 15% is good — but check whether it is achieved with heavy debt.
ROCEReturn on all capital employed, debt includedUsually more honest than ROE, because leverage cannot flatter it.
Debt/EquityHow much of the business runs on borrowed moneyCompare within the sector only.
Interest coverageHow many times operating profit covers interestBelow 3 is uncomfortable. Below 1.5 is a warning.

The one habit that matters more than any individual ratio: always compare like with like — the same company over five years, and its closest competitors in the same period. A ratio in isolation is a number without a scale.

Why cash flow settles arguments

Three cash flow shapes and what they usually indicate:

  • Operating positive, investing negative, financing negative. The business generates cash, reinvests some of it, and repays debt or pays dividends. This is the healthy pattern.
  • Operating negative, financing positive. Operations consume cash and borrowing or equity issuance funds the gap. Sustainable for a genuine growth company for a while; dangerous for a mature one.
  • Operating consistently below net profit, year after year. Reported profit is not converting to cash. This deserves a real explanation before you invest a rupee.

Comparing cumulative operating cash flow to cumulative net profit over five years is a two-minute check that filters out a surprising number of problems.

The numbers are not the whole answer

Financials tell you what happened. A few qualitative questions tell you whether it can continue:

  • What stops a competitor doing this? Brand, distribution, switching costs, regulatory licence, network effects, genuine cost advantage. If nothing stops them, margins will compress eventually.
  • Who is the promoter, and what is their record? Related-party transactions, frequent auditor changes, repeated equity dilution and a history of unmet guidance are all publicly visible and all meaningful.
  • What would break this business? Regulation, a single dominant customer, one raw material, one geography, one technology shift. Write the answer down; it becomes your exit trigger.
  • Is the sector growing? A good company in a shrinking industry is a hard place to make money.

A practical filtering routine

Free screeners let you narrow thousands of listed companies to a workable shortlist. A reasonable starting filter for quality, which you should then adjust to your own thinking:

  1. Market capitalisation above a floor you are comfortable with — smaller companies are far less liquid and far less scrutinised.
  2. Five-year sales growth and profit growth both positive.
  3. ROCE above 15% for at least three of the last five years.
  4. Debt to equity below 1 (skip this filter for financials).
  5. Promoter pledge near zero.
  6. Positive operating cash flow in at least four of the last five years.

That typically leaves a few dozen names. The screener's job ends there. The next step is reading two annual reports and one conference call transcript per company, which is where the actual work — and the actual edge — lives.

Combining fundamentals with technicals

The two disciplines answer different questions, and the most practical use is to let each do its own job:

Fundamentals decide what to own. Technicals decide when to buy it and where you are wrong.

A financially strong company can drift sideways for two years. Buying it at a technically sensible level — a well-tested support, a breakout from a long consolidation — improves your entry price and, just as importantly, gives you a defined invalidation point. Owning something with good fundamentals is not a reason to have no exit plan.

The trap to avoid "The fundamentals are strong, so I will hold through this fall" is a legitimate long-term investing position only if you have actually re-read the fundamentals since the fall started. Used as a reflex, it is the most respectable-sounding way there is to avoid taking a loss.

Where to find the data, free

  • Company websites — annual reports, quarterly results and investor presentations are all in the investor relations section, by law.
  • NSE and BSE websites — shareholding patterns, pledging disclosures, corporate announcements.
  • Earnings call transcripts — usually the highest-value document available. Analysts ask the uncomfortable questions and management's evasiveness is often more informative than the answers.

All of it is free and public. The difference between investors is almost never access to information. It is who bothers to read it.
