RSI and MACD: Using Indicators Without Fooling Yourself

What RSI and MACD actually measure, why 'overbought' does not mean 'about to fall', how to use divergence and crossovers correctly, and the filters that make indicators useful.

By the Gift City Trader team Updated 8 March 2026 13 min read

  3. MACD: a trend tool used as a momentum tool

There is a predictable arc to how traders use indicators. Month one: add RSI, buy everything below 30. Month two: it stops working, so add MACD. Month three: add Bollinger Bands, Supertrend, Stochastic and three moving averages until the chart looks like a circuit diagram and every possible action is justified by something. Month four: delete them all and declare indicators useless.

Both ends of that arc are wrong. Indicators are not signals — they are compressions of price data that make one specific property easier to see. Used for that, they are genuinely useful. Used as buy and sell triggers, they are close to random.

This guide covers what RSI and MACD actually compute, what they can and cannot tell you, and how to use them the way people who trade for a living do.

What every indicator really is

Every indicator on your chart is a formula whose only inputs are past price and volume. It cannot know anything the chart does not already contain. What it can do is make a property of that data easier for a human to perceive — the rate of change, the average over a window, the spread around a mean.

This produces two permanent limitations that no setting can fix:

  • They lag. An average of the last fourteen periods necessarily tells you about the last fourteen periods. Shortening the period reduces the lag and increases false signals. There is no setting that removes the trade-off; there is only where you choose to sit on it.
  • They are not independent. Adding RSI, Stochastic and CCI does not give you three opinions. All three measure momentum from the same prices. When they agree, it is not confirmation — it is the same calculation, three times.
The two-indicator rule Use at most one indicator per property. One for trend, one for momentum, one for volatility — never three for momentum. If two indicators would always agree, one of them is decoration.

RSI: what it measures and what it doesn't

The Relative Strength Index compares the average size of up moves to the average size of down moves over a lookback period, usually 14, and expresses the result on a 0–100 scale. High readings mean recent up-moves have been much larger than recent down-moves. That is the whole claim.

The overbought misunderstanding

RSI above 70 is conventionally called "overbought", and beginners read that as "expensive, will fall". It does not mean that. It means buying pressure has been strongly dominant. In a genuine trend that is exactly what you would expect, and it can persist for months.

A trending stock can sit above RSI 70 through a 40% rally. Shorting each time it crossed 70 would have produced an unbroken run of losses. This is the single most expensive misreading in retail technical analysis.

The correct framing depends on regime:

Market regimeHow to read RSI
Range-bound / sidewaysOverbought and oversold extremes have genuine mean-reversion value
Strong uptrendRSI rarely drops below 40; pullbacks to the 40–50 band are the buy zone, not 30
Strong downtrendRSI rarely exceeds 60; rallies into 50–60 are the sell zone, not 70

Deciding the regime first, and then reading RSI accordingly, is most of the skill.

Divergence — useful, and widely over-traded

Divergence occurs when price makes a new extreme but RSI does not. Price prints a higher high while RSI prints a lower high (bearish divergence), or price prints a lower low while RSI prints a higher low (bullish divergence). The reading is that the new extreme was achieved with less force than the previous one.

It is a real phenomenon and a poor standalone entry, for one reason: divergence tells you momentum is weakening, not that direction is about to change. A trend can diverge repeatedly and keep going. The traders who lose money on divergence are the ones who short the first one.

Conditions that make divergence worth acting on:

  • It appears at a significant support or resistance zone, not mid-range.
  • It is on the daily or higher timeframe. Intraday divergence is mostly noise.
  • Price confirms — a structure break, a strong rejection candle. The divergence is the warning; price gives the trigger.

The setting question

Default 14 is fine. Shorter periods (7–9) react faster and whipsaw more; longer periods (21–25) are smoother and slower. Changing the setting does not create an edge — it moves you along the same trade-off. Optimising it against past data almost always produces a number that fits history and fails forward.

MACD: a trend tool that people use as a momentum tool

MACD is built from two exponential moving averages, conventionally 12 and 26 periods. It has three visible parts:

  • MACD line — the 12-period EMA minus the 26-period EMA. It measures how far the fast average has separated from the slow one, which is a direct measure of trend strength.
  • Signal line — a 9-period EMA of the MACD line. A smoothed version of the above.
  • Histogram — the gap between the two lines. This is the part worth watching: it shows whether the separation is expanding or contracting.

Crossovers: the most over-traded signal in retail trading

"Buy when MACD crosses above signal" is taught everywhere and works poorly on its own, because MACD is derived from moving averages and moving averages are hopeless in a sideways market. In a range you get a crossover every few candles, each one a small loss. Over a quarter of range-bound trading, those small losses add up to a large one.

The filter that makes crossovers usable: only take crossovers in the direction of the higher-timeframe trend. If the daily is in an uptrend, take bullish crossovers on the hourly and ignore bearish ones entirely. This single rule removes most of the losing trades, at the cost of halving the number of signals — which is a very good trade.

The zero line matters more than the crossover

MACD above zero means the fast EMA is above the slow one — the definition of an uptrend on that timeframe. Below zero, a downtrend. Many experienced traders use MACD purely as a regime filter and never trade its crossovers at all: above zero, only look for longs; below zero, only look for shorts. It is a cruder use of the tool and a considerably more profitable one.

Histogram as an early warning

The histogram peaks before the crossover happens, because contraction precedes the cross. A shrinking histogram in a trending move says the trend is decelerating — useful for tightening a stop or taking partial profit. It is not a reversal signal. Deceleration and reversal are different things, and conflating them is expensive.

Combining them without deceiving yourself

A workable framework using both, on any liquid instrument:

  1. Regime, from the higher timeframe. Daily MACD above zero and price above the 200-period average? Uptrend. Only longs today.
  2. Location, from price. Wait for a pullback to a support zone you drew before the session started. Not an indicator reading — an actual level.
  3. Momentum, from RSI. In an uptrend, RSI returning to the 40–50 band during that pullback says the correction is normal, not a breakdown.
  4. Trigger, from price again. A rejection candle, or a break of the pullback's minor structure.
  5. Invalidation, from the level. Stop beyond the zone. Position size derived from that stop distance, never the other way round.

Notice the shape of that: indicators define context, price defines action. Every entry and exit decision comes from price. The indicators are there to stop you taking a good-looking setup in the wrong environment.

Six ways traders fool themselves with indicators

  1. Stacking correlated indicators. RSI, Stochastic and Williams %R agreeing is one opinion wearing three hats.
  2. Optimising settings on history. Any parameter can be tuned to look brilliant on past data. Curve-fitting is the most common cause of a backtest that dies live.
  3. Trading indicator signals without a level. A crossover in the middle of a range has no invalidation point, so there is no sensible stop and no sensible size.
  4. Adding an indicator after a loss. The loss was almost never caused by a missing indicator. Adding one just delays the real diagnosis.
  5. Using default settings on every timeframe. RSI(14) on a 1-minute chart is measuring fourteen minutes of noise. Match the lookback to how long your trades last.
  6. Believing the indicator over the price. If RSI says oversold and price keeps falling on rising volume, price is right. It always is — it is the thing being measured.

The honest summary

Indicators do not predict. They describe, with a delay, a property of data you already have. Their real value is as filters: tools that tell you which environment you are in, so you stop taking mean-reversion trades in a trending market and momentum trades in a range.

Two indicators, understood deeply and used as context, beat eight indicators used as signals. And neither beats a clear level, a defined invalidation and a position size you can live with.
