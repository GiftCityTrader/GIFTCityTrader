Support and Resistance, Explained Properly

Why price levels exist, how to draw only the levels that matter, the three ways to trade them, and why false breakouts happen so often on Indian charts.

By the Gift City Trader team Updated 19 February 2026 12 min read

Support and resistance is the first concept taught in every technical analysis course and the one most consistently misused. The textbook version — "support is where price bounces, resistance is where price stops" — is circular. It describes what happened after the fact and gives you nothing to act on before it happens.

The useful version starts with a different question: why would anyone care about that particular price? Once you can answer that for a level, you can judge whether it will hold, and you can size a trade around it sensibly.

Why levels exist at all

A price level matters because of the people who transacted there. Three groups create the pressure:

  • Traders who bought there and are now in profit. If price returns, some will add. Buying pressure.
  • Traders who bought there and are now in loss. If price returns to their entry, many exit at breakeven out of relief. Selling pressure.
  • Traders who watched the move and missed it. If price returns, they finally get the entry they wanted. Buying pressure.

A support level is simply a price where the first and third groups outweigh the second. Nothing mystical. And this immediately explains a fact that confuses beginners: a broken support becomes resistance. Everyone who bought at that level is now trapped in a loss. When price crawls back up to it, they sell to escape — and the level that once absorbed selling now generates it.

Levels are not lines on a chart. They are collections of unresolved decisions.

How to draw levels that are worth drawing

Most beginner charts have twenty lines on them. Twenty lines mean price is always "near a level", which means the concept has stopped being informative. A useful chart has three to five.

Start on the higher timeframe

Open the weekly chart first, then the daily, then your trading timeframe. Levels drawn on higher timeframes are visible to more participants and therefore attract more orders. A level that is obvious on the weekly chart will be respected on the 15-minute chart; a level drawn only on the 15-minute chart is invisible to everyone trading the daily.

Mark zones, not lines

Price rarely reverses at an exact number. Draw a rectangle covering the cluster of highs or lows rather than a single line through them. If four swing lows sit between ₹418 and ₹423, the support is that band, not ₹420.50. Thinking in zones stops you being stopped out by a two-rupee overshoot that meant nothing.

Prioritise these, in order

  1. Swing highs and lows where price reversed sharply. The sharper the reversal, the more decisive the participation.
  2. Levels tested multiple times. Three touches with clean reactions beats one.
  3. The origin of a large impulsive move. Where a big candle started, an imbalance of orders existed. Those areas are often revisited and defended.
  4. Round numbers on indices. Nifty at 24,000, Bank Nifty at 56,000 — option strikes cluster there, which makes them genuinely self-fulfilling.
  5. Prior day / week / month high and low. Especially useful intraday, because almost every intraday participant has them on screen.
Test your own levels Draw them on the left half of a chart with the right half hidden, then scroll forward. If your levels do not produce visible reactions, you are drawing where price went, not where it decided.

Dynamic support and resistance

Not all levels are horizontal.

Trendlines

A line connecting successive higher lows (or lower highs). Genuinely useful, and genuinely easy to abuse — with enough willingness you can fit a trendline to any chart. Rules that keep you honest: require at least three touches, draw from candle bodies or wicks consistently rather than switching to suit your bias, and never redraw a line just because price broke it.

Moving averages

In a strong trend, price often pulls back to a moving average and resumes. The 20 EMA works on fast trends, the 50 on medium swings, the 200 as a long-term regime marker. These work not because of the mathematics but because a large number of participants watch the same averages and place orders around them.

VWAP

Volume Weighted Average Price is the intraday level that institutional desks actually measure themselves against — a buyer filling a large order wants an average price below VWAP. That gives it real order flow behind it, which is why intraday reactions at VWAP are often clean.

Trading a level: the two honest approaches

There are exactly two ways to trade a level, and they suit different temperaments.

The bounce (mean reversion)

You expect the level to hold. Price approaches support, you wait for evidence that sellers are being absorbed — a rejection candle, a slowdown in downward momentum, a volume spike on the reversal — and you enter with a stop just beyond the zone.

Advantage: your stop is tight, so risk-reward can be excellent. Disadvantage: you are betting against current momentum, so you will be wrong often. This approach needs a high tolerance for small losses.

The break (momentum)

You expect the level to fail. Price pushes through resistance on strong volume and you enter in the direction of the break.

Advantage: you are trading with momentum, and the winners can run a long way. Disadvantage: false breakouts are extremely common, especially in the first fifteen minutes of the Indian session.

The compromise: the retest

Wait for the break, then wait again for price to come back and hold the broken level from the other side. You give up some of the move in exchange for a much better entry price, a tighter stop, and confirmation that the break was real. For most part-time traders this is the highest-quality version of the setup, and it is worth learning to be patient enough to take only these.

Why false breakouts happen so often

Stop-loss orders cluster just beyond obvious levels — everybody puts their stop "a little below support". A cluster of stops is a pool of pending orders, and pending orders are liquidity. Large participants who need to fill size have a genuine incentive to push price into that pool, absorb the resulting flow, and then trade in the opposite direction.

Whether you call that manipulation or ordinary liquidity-seeking, the practical response is the same:

  • Place stops beyond the zone, with room, rather than at the round number where everyone else has theirs.
  • Require a candle close beyond the level, not just a wick through it.
  • Prefer the retest entry over the break entry.
  • Treat a strong rejection back into the range after a break as a signal in its own right — a failed breakout often produces a fast move the other way.

Reading volume alongside levels

What you seeReasonable reading
Approach to support on falling volumeSelling is drying up — support more likely to hold
Approach to support on rising volumeActive distribution — break more likely
Breakout with a large volume expansionGenuine participation; break more likely to hold
Breakout on flat or low volumeClassic false-break profile; wait for the retest
Long lower wick with volume spike at supportAbsorption — the strongest bounce signal there is

Volume is not a separate indicator to add to the chart. It is the confirmation layer that tells you whether the price action at a level was backed by real participation or was just drift.

Mistakes that cost money

  1. Too many levels. Three to five per chart. If everything is a level, nothing is.
  2. Entering without confirmation. "Price is at support" is not a trade. "Price is at support and sellers were just rejected on volume" is.
  3. Moving the stop. The level defines your invalidation. Widening the stop as price approaches it converts a planned small loss into an unplanned large one.
  4. Forgetting the trend. Support fails far more often in a downtrend. Counter-trend bounces need better evidence and smaller size.
  5. Ignoring events. Levels are ordinary-conditions tools. Around results, RBI policy or a budget, order flow is driven by information, not by chart memory.

A ten-minute weekend routine

This is the whole practice, and it takes less time than one episode of anything:

  1. Open the weekly chart of your instrument. Mark two or three major zones.
  2. Drop to the daily. Mark two or three more. Delete anything that now looks arbitrary.
  3. Note where price is relative to those zones — mid-range, approaching, or breaking out.
  4. Write one line for the week ahead: "Long only above X, short only below Y, no trade in between."

That single sentence prevents more bad trades than any indicator you could add to the chart.
