Option Greeks Explained: Why You Can Be Right on Direction and Still Lose

Delta, gamma, theta and vega explained in plain language for Indian index and stock options — including IV crush, expiry-day gamma, and the rules that follow from each.

By the Gift City Trader team Updated 27 March 2026 14 min read

Most people who lose money in options do not lose it because they got the direction wrong. They lose it because they were right about direction and still watched the premium fall. The stock went up, the call option went down, and nothing in the chart explained it.

The explanation is always the Greeks. An option's price responds to at least four things at once — where the underlying is, how much time is left, how volatile the market expects things to be, and interest rates. The Greeks are simply the names given to each of those sensitivities. You do not need the mathematics. You do need the intuition, because without it options trading is guessing with extra steps.

Before this guide It assumes you know what a call, a put, a strike price, a premium and an expiry are. If any of those are shaky, start with the market basics guide first — Greeks on top of unclear basics will not stick.

Where an option premium comes from

Every premium splits into exactly two parts:

Intrinsic value is what the option would be worth if it expired right now. A 24,000 call with Nifty at 24,150 has 150 points of intrinsic value. If Nifty were at 23,900, intrinsic value would be zero — never negative, because you would simply not exercise.

Time value (or extrinsic value) is everything else — what the market is willing to pay for the possibility that things move favourably before expiry. If that 24,000 call trades at 210 with Nifty at 24,150, then 150 is intrinsic and 60 is time value.

At expiry, time value is zero by definition. Every option converges to its intrinsic value. That one sentence explains most option losses: buyers pay for time value and time value always, without exception, goes to zero.

Delta — direction, and a rough probability

Delta measures how much the option premium moves for a one-point move in the underlying.

A call with delta 0.45 gains roughly ₹0.45 in premium when the underlying rises by ₹1. Calls have deltas from 0 to 1; puts from 0 to -1 (they gain when the underlying falls).

Option positionTypical deltaBehaviour
Deep in-the-money call0.85 – 1.00Moves nearly like the stock itself
At-the-money call~0.50Moves about half as fast as the stock
Far out-of-the-money call0.05 – 0.20Barely responds unless the move is large

Delta as approximate probability

A widely used rule of thumb: delta roughly approximates the probability of the option expiring in-the-money. A 0.20 delta call has, very loosely, a 20% chance of finishing in the money.

This reframes the most common beginner trade in Indian markets — buying far out-of-the-money weekly options because they are cheap. A ₹4 option is cheap because the market assesses roughly a 5% chance it finishes in the money. It is not a bargain; it is correctly priced insurance against something unlikely. Buy enough of them and the arithmetic is relentless.

Gamma — why delta will not stay still

Gamma measures how fast delta itself changes as the underlying moves.

Delta is not constant. As price rises, a call's delta rises too. Gamma is the rate of that change, and it is the reason options can feel calm and then violent within the same session.

Two properties matter practically:

  • Gamma is highest at the money, and drops off in both directions. Around the current spot price, small moves change delta a lot.
  • Gamma rises sharply as expiry approaches. On expiry day, at-the-money options have enormous gamma — delta can swing from 0.3 to 0.7 on a thirty-point index move.

This is exactly why expiry-day trading in Nifty and Bank Nifty feels different from any other day. Positions that looked hedged at 10 a.m. are directionally exposed by 2 p.m. because delta moved underneath them. Option sellers face this in its most dangerous form: a short at-the-money option on expiry day accumulates directional risk faster than most people can manage it. High gamma cuts both ways, and it is unforgiving of inattention.

Theta — the cost of waiting

Theta measures how much premium is lost with the passage of one day, all else equal.

Theta is negative for option buyers and positive for sellers. If an option shows theta of -8, it loses about ₹8 of value per day if nothing else changes.

Decay is not linear

This is the part that surprises people. Time value does not bleed away evenly. It decays slowly early in the option's life and accelerates sharply in the final week — and dramatically in the final day or two.

Consequences for Indian weekly index options, where most retail activity sits:

  • A weekly option bought on Monday and held to Thursday faces the steepest part of the decay curve.
  • Being right about direction but early is often a losing trade. Theta charges rent for every day you wait.
  • Buying an option and holding it "in case it comes back" is the single most expensive habit in options, because the position is losing value even when price does nothing.
The seller's temptation Positive theta makes selling options feel like collecting a salary — most weeks, it works. The risk is not that it fails often; it is that when it fails, it can fail for many multiples of the premium collected. Selling naked options without a defined hedge is how otherwise careful traders lose a year of gains in one session.

Vega — the Greek that ambushes beginners

Vega measures how much the premium changes for a one-point change in implied volatility.

Implied volatility (IV) is the market's collective expectation of how much the underlying will move. It is not a prediction of direction and it is not measured from past prices — it is derived from what people are currently willing to pay for options.

Here is the scenario that catches almost everyone at least once:

Results are due tomorrow. IV is elevated because everyone expects a large move. You buy a call. The results are good, the stock gaps up 4% — and your call is worth less than you paid. The uncertainty that justified the high IV has resolved, IV collapses, and the vega loss exceeds the delta gain.

This is IV crush, and it happens reliably around scheduled events: company results, RBI policy, budget day, election counting. Buying options into a known event means paying for volatility that is guaranteed to disappear the moment the event passes.

Practical rules:

  • Check IV relative to its own history before buying. High IV means expensive options.
  • Long options benefit from rising IV; short options benefit from falling IV.
  • Around events, prefer structures that are less exposed to IV — spreads, where you buy one option and sell another, so the vega largely cancels out.

Rho — and why you can mostly ignore it

Rho measures sensitivity to interest rates. For short-dated Indian index options, its effect is negligible next to delta, gamma, theta and vega. Know it exists; spend your attention elsewhere.

Reading the Greeks as one picture

The Greeks do not act separately. Every position is a package:

PositionDeltaGammaThetaVega
Long callPositivePositiveNegativePositive
Long putNegativePositiveNegativePositive
Short callNegativeNegativePositiveNegative
Short putPositiveNegativePositiveNegative

Read the long call row as a sentence: I profit if price rises, I profit more as the move accelerates, I lose money every day that passes, and I benefit if the market becomes more nervous. That sentence is the trade. If you cannot say it about a position before you enter, you do not understand what you are holding.

Three quick applications

  • You expect a large move within two days. Delta and gamma work for you; theta works against you. A short holding period is essential — this is not a position to sit on.
  • You expect the market to go nowhere. Theta is your income and gamma is your enemy. Define the risk with a spread rather than selling naked.
  • You expect volatility to rise but do not know the direction. Vega is what you are trading. Delta-neutral structures exist for exactly this, and they are their own discipline to learn.

Practical rules that follow from all this

  1. Never buy an option without knowing when you will be out. Theta makes "hold and hope" mathematically expensive.
  2. Check IV before every option purchase. Buying high IV means needing to be right about direction and timing and magnitude.
  3. Prefer nearer-the-money strikes for directional views. Cheap far-OTM options are cheap because they usually expire worthless.
  4. Respect expiry-day gamma. Reduce size, not increase it, on the day risk is highest.
  5. Define the maximum loss before entry. With spreads it is a known number. With naked shorts it is not, and "not" is not a risk-management plan.

Understanding the Greeks will not make you profitable on its own. What it does is remove an entire category of loss — the one where the market moved your way and you lost money anyway, without knowing why.
